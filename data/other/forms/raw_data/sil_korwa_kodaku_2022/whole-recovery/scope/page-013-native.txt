1.3.1 
The Korwa 
The Korwa people are mainly divided into two distinct groups, the Pahadi Korwa and the Dihari 
Korwa. The ones who still live in the hills and forest are called Pahadi/Pahariya Korwa (‘hill 
dwellers’) and the ones who came down and settled in the plain areas are called Dihari/Dihariya 
Korwa (‘plains dwellers’). They are not mutually dependent on each other. Their lifestyle, 
occupation, culture, etc. distinguish them as separate communities. The Dihari Korwa people live in a 
village situation, while the Pahadi Korwa people usually live in the? hills and forests. Occupationally, 
the Dihari Korwa people are agriculturists living in the plains, whereas the Pahadi Korwas people are 
food gatherers and basket makers who live in the hills, usually covered by dense forests (Narayan 
1990:18). The Dihari Korwa people are developed farmers and economically stabilized, while the 
Pahadi Korwa people are still economically poor. 
In this study, the main focus is given to the Pahadi Korwa people group and their language. 
Socially, the Pahadi Korwa people are divided into five totemist endogamous clans which they call 
Gotra, with a specific totem for each:4 Hazeda ‘bamboo’, Edigwar-Kachi ‘a creeper’, Samat ‘a tree’, 
Madhiya-Dohs ‘head’, and Ginu ‘ant hill soil’ (CTDP 2002:n.p.). It is forbidden to marry within the 
same clan or with another caste, and Pahadi Korwa and Dihari Korwa do not intermarry. The system 
of bride price, remarriage, child marriage, and polygyny is commonly practiced. 
Korwa marriage practices are typical for India (J.P., p.c. 2004). First, the bridegroom proposes 
to the bride’s family members. Agreeing to a proposal is called mangni. If the date is fixed, after few 
weeks or months, there is a feast known as Hadia, during which they drink the hadia beverage. Then, 
after fifteen days another feast is held, called Panchbandhi. On this occasion, the families decide to 
conduct the marriage on a particular day determined to be an auspicious or “lucky day” for 
marriage. According to custom, the marriage is conducted in the bride’s house. On the day of the 
wedding, there is a puja path (‘ritual’) called Pitra Puja. Following that there is a feast and a 
celebration which includes applying turmeric to each other and dancing while singing a traditional 
marriage song in the Korwa language. After the wedding, there is a programme called Chuman, 
during which all their relatives give the newlyweds clothes, household vessels, etc. After that 
programme the newlyweds go to the groom’s house. 
1.3.2 
The Kodaku 
Compared to the Pahadi Korwa people, the Kodaku people receive less aid from the government. 
According to Singh (1994:545), the Kodaku people are often grouped with Korwa. The Kodaku 
people say, however, they do not like to be grouped with the Korwa people because it hides their 
identity as a separate people group. 
The Kodaku people have a community council with a mahato as the village headman. The post 
of mahato is hereditary. They are divided into a number of clans such as Kumharia or Kumbaria, 
Palhe, Charghat, Haddae, Dumaria, and Sommat or Sammat. 
Marriage is generally arranged through negotiation, by mutual consent. They mostly practise 
monogamy; however polygamy is also practiced by some people. A bride price is required, which is 
paid to the bride’s parents in cash and goods. One of the marriage rituals is to circle around the fire 
five times. Divorce can be sought by either spouse on grounds of barrenness or adultery. There is no 
bar for remarriage for either the man or the woman (Singh 1994:546). 
Among the Kodaku both nuclear and extended families are found. In the family, they recognize 
birth purification, a ceremony that lasts for one day. The naming ceremony called chatti is celebrated 
on the sixth day. They also practice death purification, which they observe for ten days. The dead are 
generally buried, although they are sometimes cremated (J.P., p.c. 2004). 
1.3.3 
Education 
Education was previously unattainable for both the Pahadi Korwa and the Kodaku people. Owing to 
poverty, most of them are illiterate. Their children rarely studied beyond the primary school level.
