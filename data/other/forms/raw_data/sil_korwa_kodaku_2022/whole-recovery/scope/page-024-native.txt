3.1.1 
Results among the Korwa 
The Pahadi Korwa wordlists range from 71–92 percent in lexical similarity to each other. The 
Mudskhorl variety shows the lowest similarity (71–76%) compared with 76–85 percent to the other 
Korwa wordlists. All other Korwa varieties show relatively close similarity to each other (83–92%). 
The Rakkaya wordlist has slightly higher lexical similarity percentages (73–92%) to the other Pahadi 
Korwa varieties. These findings suggest raise the question whether, in terms of lexical similarity, 
Rakkaya could be considered the central variety. 
The lexical similarity between Pahadi Korwa and Sadri ranges from 21–38 percent. The local 
Sadri variety (spoken by the Dihari Korwa from Kotagohna) shows only 26–36 percent similarity to 
the Korwa language. Thus, Pahadi Korwa and Sadri may be considered separate languages, as far as 
their lexical similarity is concerned. 
3.1.2 
Results among the Kodaku 
The lexical similarity percentages of the Kodaku wordlists range from 82–96 percent. The Kodaku 
wordlists from Baikanthpur (CG) and Chainpur (UP) have the highest lexical similarity to the other 
Kodaku varieties (88–96%). These two varieties are geographically close to each other and are 
considered the same variety. The Kodaku variety of Baikanthpur, however, has shares slightly higher 
lexical similarity percentages with the other Kodaku varieties, than Chainpur does. This, in turn, 
raises the question whether Baikanthpur could be considered the central variety of the Kodaku 
language. 
3.1.3 
Results between the Korwa and Kodaku languages 
The lexical similarity percentages between the Korwa and Kodaku wordlists range from 50–70 
percent. The wordlists from Chilma (Korwa) and Sagardinwa (Kodaku) show the highest degree of 
similarity (70%). The reason for this may be that the researchers, while eliciting the Kodaku wordlist 
in Sagardinwa, acquired more synonyms based on the Korwa wordlist from Chilma. As a result, these 
two wordlists might have more similar words than the other wordlists. 
If the Sagardinwa wordlist is not included in the comparison, then the lexical similarity 
percentages between Korwa and Kodaku ranges drop to 50–65 percent. These low lexical similarity 
percentages between the Korwa and Kodaku varieties suggests that they are different languages. 
3.1.4 
Result among other languages 
Other Austro-Asiatic languages compared in this survey range from 33–53 percent in lexical 
similarity with Korwa and Kodaku. They may be considered to be different languages from Korwa 
and Kodaku since they fall below the threshold of 60 percent lexical similarity. 
The variety spoken in Tanmai, Jharkhand, is said to be a Korwa dialect. However, its lexical 
similarity percentages with the other Korwa varieties are rather low (53–60%), suggesting that 
Tanmai is a separate language from Korwa. It is a separate language from Kodaku and Sadri as well. 
The lexical similarity percentages betweenthe two Local Sadri varieties and Standard Sadri are 
73 and 75 percent. 
3.2 
Dialect intelligibility 
Since the Kodaku language spoken in Tanmai, Jharkhand, is referred to by local speakers as Korwa, 
the researchers felt the need to further verify the findings from the lexical similarity study, which 
suggest that these two varieties are different languages, or at least very distinctive varieties. To verify 
this, intelligibility testing was carried out in Jharkhand. Both Kodaku and Korwa stories were 
collected from Chhattisgarh and played in Chakri village, one of the Kodaku speaking villages in 
Jharkhand (see map 4). The Kodaku text from Kanda village in Chhattisgarh served as the practice 
test; a separate Hometown Test (HTT) was not developed. The details about these stories and the 
recorded text test (RTT) procedure are explained in section 4 and section 5.3. Table 5 shows the 
results from the intelligibility testing.
