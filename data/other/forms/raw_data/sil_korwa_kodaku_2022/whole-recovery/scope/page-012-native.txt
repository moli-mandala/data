The Korwa people are said to be the original inhabitants of Korba and Surguja districts of 
Chhattisgarh.3 They were believed to be Zamindars (landlords) and Rajas (kings) who built forts and 
were ruling the land. In the early 1900s, they were dubbed as the leaders and chiefs of this region 
until powerful conquerors invaded them. For example, a king named Ghughus, belonging to the 
Korwa community, ruled over Ratanpur. After that, the Rajputs took over the kingdom, and later this 
was taken over by the British (Powercity Korba n.d.b). It is believed that during the British reign, the 
Korwa kingdom was attacked and collapsed. According to the tales of the hills when the Korwa king 
lost to the British, he along with his subjects fled to the jungles of Korea Jhadi (Korea hills) 
(Powercity Korba n.d.a). 
The Korwa lived in and around the hills and forest for a long time, struggling for bare essentials 
such as food, clothing, and shelter. Gradually this situation became the basis for their simple 
lifestyle. This isolation forced them to develop their own means of survival. However, gradually their 
isolation has been broken and so has their identity. As a result of their contact with the outside 
world, their desire for further development is increasing (Mohan 1993:12–22). 
1.2.2 
The Kodaku 
Very little is known about the history of the Kodaku people. However, in the notification list of the 
Scheduled Tribes in India, they have been grouped with the Korwa people (Singh 1994:545). Like the 
Korwa people, the Kodaku people also consider themselves to be the successors of the Kauravs of 
Mahabharata, and their lifestyle and culture are also comparable to that of the Pandos, a 
neighbouring tribal group. They are a nomadic hill tribe, who earned their livelihood from the forest. 
They used to clear the forests for seasonal cultivation, and others were getting the benefits of it. 
However, gradually they began to live in the plains and were influenced by outsiders, especially by 
Christian missionaries, who have helped them in different ways. 
1.3 
People 
Both the Korwa and the Kodaku people are tribal communities who live an isolated life in the hills, 
valleys, and forest regions. Access to their settlements by outsiders is often impossible. However, they 
seem to be generally happy in their secluded lifestyle in their settlements. Both tribes are 
distinguished from others by their isolated lifestyle and physical appearance. Generally they are short 
in height with dark brown complexions (CTDP 2002:n.p.). 
Their primary occupation is food-gathering and agriculture. Few of them possess cultivatable 
land, but a major portion of it has been mortgaged to the neighbouring tribal and non-tribal 
communities. Generally, they manage their livelihood by daily wage labour and selling collected 
forest products in the local market (CTDP 2002:n.p.). The women, besides attending to household 
chores, take part in agricultural and non-agricultural labour. 
The main staple of the Korwa and Kodaku is rice and wheat. They are not vegetarians. They eat 
a variety of leafy vegetables and pulses. Drinking an indigenous alcohol, called hadia, made from the 
mahua flower and fermented rice, as well as smoking and chewing tobacco are among their habits. 
They do not eat with other castes or tribes. However, they accept water and cooked food from 
neighbours such as the Yadav, Bania, Gond, Kanwar, and Kharwar (Singh 1994:547). 
There are no separate population figures available for the Korwa and Kodaku (Singh 1994:545). 
According to the 1981 census the population of these two tribes was 25,502 in Chhattisgarh and 
21,940 in Jharkhand (Dubey 1987:486). By the 1991 census (RGCCI 1991) their population had 
increased to 85,585 in Chhattisgarh and 24.871 in Jharkhand. The Ethnologue (Gordon 2005) 
recorded the Korwa population as 66,000 in 1997 and does not give any figures for Kodaku speakers. 
Based on the researchers’ compilation from different government documents from 2002–2005, 
the population of Korwa is estimated at 31,300 in Chhattisgarh (10,700 in Jashpur district and 
20,600 in Surguja district), and 18,400 in Jharkhand (excluding the population from Palamau and 
Gumla districts).
