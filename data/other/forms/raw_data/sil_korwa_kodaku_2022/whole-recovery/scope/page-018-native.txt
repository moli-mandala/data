Figure 1. The language attitude responses among the Pahadi Korwa. 
The attitude of the Speaking Pahadi Korwa subjects toward their mother tongue is very positive. 
All of them said that they would like their children to read and write in the Korwa language. In 
addition, most of them also believe that their language will continue to be used by the coming 
generation. A little less than three-quarters of the subjects said that they would not be happy if their 
children spoke only Hindi or Sadri. However, only 63 percent of them reported Korwa to be their 
favourite language. Furthermore, when asked whether they allow marriage with a Pahadi Korwa 
person who does not speak the Korwa language, most of them reported that such a marriage is 
acceptable. However, they also expressed a desire that the person should be from the Korwa 
community because they do not encourage marrying from outside their tribe. This reflects that the 
mother tongue does not play an important role among the Pahadi Korwa in terms of their marriage 
preferences and shows their flexibility toward their language. 
The attitude of the Shifting Pahadi Korwa to the Korwa language seems to be strong only in the 
case of vernacular education for their children. However, this response contradicts their language use 
pattern in general: they use Sadri extensively in all domains. The respondents themselves regret not 
being able to speak Korwa, but it is their desire that Korwa be taught to their children. With this in 
mind, more than half of them expressed the expectation that their next generation would speak the 
Korwa language, if someone would teach them. A little more than one-quarter of them are not happy 
that their children speak only Sadri. Other responses reveal that the use of the Korwa language is not 
at all important in marriage. All respondents reported that they would allow marriage with someone 
whether they speak the Korwa language or not. Only 13 percent of them said that their favourite 
language is Korwa, and this response was mostly from very old subjects. Overall, it is found that 
while those who are no longer using Korwa retain a positive attitude toward language development, 
Sadri is the preferred language in actual usage. 
Apart from the above responses, other answers reflect the respondents’ attitudes toward their 
language. A question, ‘If young people in your village did not speak Korwa, how would you feel?’, 
was asked to both young and old subjects. Among Speaking Pahadi Korwa, most older subjects 
expressed that they would be unhappy if their young people did not speak their language. Young 
people also responded that older people feel bad if they do not speak the Korwa language. On the 
other hand, when this same question was asked among Shifting Pahadi Korwa, the response suggest 
that it does not bother the old people whether their young people speak the Korwa language or not. 
In fact, one 80-year-old subject expressed that he is not sad for young people who are not able to 
speak the Korwa language because he himself does not know how to speak the language. Overall 
observations supported this finding.
