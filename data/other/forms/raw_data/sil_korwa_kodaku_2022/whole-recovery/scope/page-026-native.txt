Sadri, also an Indo-Aryan language, is spoken as a mother tongue by numerous Adivasi groups 
and castes spread across India and Bangladesh. It also serves as a language of wider communication 
in these areas (Abraham 1995:11). Although Chhattisgarhi is the state language of Chhattisgarh and 
Hindi is the official language of both Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand (Hindi is, for example, the medium 
for instruction in schools, and is used primarily for government purposes). Sadri has a great influence 
in northern Chhattisgarh and southwestern Jharkhand. Tribes in these areas use Sadri more than 
Hindi. As mentioned earlier, some of these tribes even speak Sadri as their first language (1995:13). 
On the other hand, it is the second language of some tribes, especially tribes such as the Korwa and 
the Kodaku, while Hindi is a third language. 
The variety of Sadri spoken in Jharkhand and Orissa is being promoted and developed. People 
often consider this variety to be the standard. Abraham (1995:11) reports that the language 
development efforts in the Sadri language have been widespread; there is literature available in this 
variety. Sadri speakers, as well as the researchers, reported seeing Sadri books, listening to Sadri 
language cassettes and CDs, and hearing radio programs in Sadri. Books written in the Sadri 
language include an English-Sadri dictionary (Blain 1975), religious books, history books, especially 
developed in Jharsuguda or Rourkela, Orissa. There is a Sadri radio program which broadcasts from 
Ranchi, Jharkhand. According to Masica (1991:442), “Sadri language development is expected to 
continue. It is particularly active in Ranchi, Jharkhand, where the Sadri language is offered as a 
subject at the University of Ranchi.” Furthermore, in this modern age, media (audio and video) have 
played a significant role in propagating the wide use of Sadri. With these factors in mind, the 
researchers determined they should investigate Sadri bilingualism rather than Hindi bilingualism. 
4.2 
Recorded text test (RTT) 
For bilingualism testing, the primary method is the sentence repetition test (SRT). However, since a 
Sadri SRT has not been developed, the researchers opted to use the RTT for exploring Sadri 
bilingualism among Korwa and Kodaku speakers. An RTT, however, is used to discern the 
intelligibility among related speech varieties. It is not a common method for testing bilingualism as it 
cannot determine bilingual proficiency at higher levels; at the most, it can confirm low proficiency 
levels. In addition to the RTT, a few questions regarding testees’ self-reported abilities in Sadri were 
included as part of the post-RTT questionnaire. The researchers’ observations in the field also helped 
to evaluate Sadri bilingualism levels among the Korwa and Kodaku groups. 
4.2.1 
Procedure 
An RTT can be used to provide a preliminary assessment of a community’s abilities in a second 
language. The procedures for evaluating testees’ bilingual abilities using this method are the same as 
those used for a dialect intelligibility testing. As this method only evaluates the comprehension 
ability of simple narratives, it is not adequate for evaluating higher levels of bilingual proficiency 
(Blair 1990:74). 
When an RTT is used for evaluating bilingualism, more subjects are tested compared with 
intelligibility testing. But, in the case of this survey, the willingness of people to be tested was quite 
low. Researchers spent considerable time explaining the purpose of the test. Some subjects demanded 
money in exchange for being tested. 
When conducting any test, care should be taken to test a sample that represents the 
demographic characteristics of the community, namely men and women, young and old, and 
educated and uneducated. However, in this survey, it was difficult to find a sufficient number of 
subjects from each of these categories.
