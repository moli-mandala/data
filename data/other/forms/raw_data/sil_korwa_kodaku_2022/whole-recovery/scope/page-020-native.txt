2.4 
Kodaku language attitudes 
In the questionnaire, a few questions were included to explore respondents’ attitudes  toward their 
mother tongue and other languages. As in the language use study, a total of 85 subjects, 44 from 
Chhattisgarh and 41 from Jharkhand, gave responses. A summary of their responses is analysed in 
figure 2. The Kodaku language responses included some that called their mother tongue Korwa. 
 
Figure 2. Responses to the Kodaku language attitude questionnaire  
in Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand. 
Figure 2 compares the language attitude responses from the Kodaku respondents in Chhattisgarh 
and those in Jharkhand. In Chhattisgarh, most of the subjects reported that the Kodaku children 
begin to speak Kodaku first and they would like their children to read and write in their mother 
tongue. However, when asked about the continuation of their language by the next generation, only 
a little more than half of them thought that it would be continued. When asked whether they allow 
their children to marry other Kodaku who do not speak their language, almost all respondents 
expressed that they allow this. However, they added that marriage with outsiders is strictly not 
allowed. In addition, 64 percent of them would reportedly be unhappy if their children spoke only 
Sadri or Hindi instead of their mother tongue, Kodaku. Altogether, the Kodaku respondents in 
Chhattisgarh have a positive attitude toward their mother tongue. 
In Jharkhand, almost every subject responded that they would like their children to read and 
write in their mother tongue. It does not necessarily equate with a strong positive attitude to their 
mother tongue because the other responses reveal that none of their children speak Kodaku first; 
rather, they mostly speak Sadri (see table 3). Furthermore, only a little more than half of them 
believe that their language will be spoken by the next generation. Only around one-quarter expressed 
that they would be unhappy if their children speak Sadri or Hindi alone or if their young people do 
not speak their mother tongue. For others (three-quarters) it does not make any difference whether 
their children or young people continue to speak their language. When asked about their favourite 
language, only a little more than half of them mentioned Kodaku. For most of them, language does 
not play a big role in marriage selection: all of them said that marriage with another Kodaku is 
permitted, even if the person does not speak Kodaku. 
From the above findings, it can be concluded that the attitude toward the Kodaku language in 
Jharkhand seems to be comparatively weaker than that in Chhattisgarh. However, informal 
conversations and observations revealed that the Kodaku people would be happy if their mother 
tongue was being developed. The majority of such respondents were older people who are aware of 
the language shift that is in progress but feel helpless to stop it.
