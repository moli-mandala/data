004: 2.2 Sociolinguistic aspects 
005: The relation between the kinship terms and the grammar of a language is 
006: pointed out by Abbi (2001:222): 
007: The intricate hierarchical society of India is reflected in its linguistic patterns 
008: and in the various terms that one uses to address and refer to different 
009: relations. As we have already seen, grammatical patterns indicate the 
010: hierarchical composition of the society (the verb endings in Hindi and 
011: Maithili, or the pronominals in Santhali, to name but a few). In addition to the 
012: grammar, one can witness the composition, directionality and intimacy of 
013: social behavior of a speech community through kinship terms. 
014: The following paragraphs contain a brief sociolinguistic profile of Darai. 
015: Further research is needed for a full treatment of Darai sociolinguistics.  
016:  
017: 2.2.1 Kinship terms 
018: The kinship system is extremely patrilineal.  Residence is patrilocal with 
019: daughters going to live at the residence of their husbands after marriage. Sister-
020: exchange marriage is not allowed. In several kinship terms, there is a distinction made 
021: if the person is referred to by honorific pronouns or not. Honorificity is decided by the 
022: speaker's relation (whether non-affinal or affinal) to the addressee. Kinship terms have 
023: been presented in this chapter following Abbi (2001).  
024:  
025: i. Non-affinal 
026:  
027: The following non-affinal kinship terms were obtained. These terms show 
028: non-affinal relations, those that an ego acquires by birth and not because of entering 
029: into a marriage alliance.  
030:  
031: a. Great grand kinsmen 
032:  
033: They are kinsmen more than two generations distant from ego. The terms 
034: obtained are:  
035: (1) 
036: adzebadze 
037: ‘great grandfather’ (father’s and mother’s side)
