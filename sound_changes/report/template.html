Key findings

  • One family-wide skeleton of relative chronology comes out of the data. Across all 151 chains, the order of pairs of changes was tested by moving each rule past the other. The cluster assimilations and sibilant merger come first. Intervocalic lenition follows, then degemination and compensatory lengthening, then loss of final vowels. For example, intervocalic stop loss must precede degemination in 120 lects with none against, and compensatory lengthening must precede apocope in 40 lects with 2 against.
  • Lenition before cluster reduction holds in every branch. What differs is how clusters reduce. Mainland MIA made geminates, which resisted lenition and simplified much later. Dardic, Nuristani and (partly) Romani never geminated, but their clusters still simplified only after lenition (40 lects for, 4 against). This is the clearest structural split between Dardic and the mainland.
  • The MIA stages separate cleanly. Pali and Aśokan reject every Prakrit lenition (adding intervocalic stop loss costs +0.058 NED). The Kharoṣṭhī texts keep all three sibilants and lenite only by voicing, in decreasing degree from the Gāndhārī Dharmapada to the Niya documents to the inscriptions. Sinhala and Dhivehi start from a Pali-like stage and develop their own lenition.
  • Where compensatory lengthening falls is a major isogloss. It is penultimate in Hindi, Pahari, Gujarati and Bengali; initial in Marathi, the Bhil lects, Bihari, Assamese and Odia; absent in the Northwest, Dardic, Insular and Romani. Adding it to any Northwest lect costs +0.03 to +0.07 NED.
  • Several classic subgroupings fall out of the rule files without being built in. Examples: Kamarupi (Assamese, Kamta, Rajbanshi, Surjapuri, Hajong, Bishnupriya) by ḍ > r; Central vs Eastern Pahari; Shina proper by tr > ʦ̣; the Northwest by no lengthening + post-nasal voicing + rt > ṭṭ; the Bhil lects as Gujarati–Rajasthani rather than Marathi; Halbi and Bhatri as central/eastern rather than Marathi.
  • Romani left the Central continuum early and then spent time in the Northwest. Grafting the Hindi MIA block onto any Romani chain costs +0.054 to +0.087 NED. Romani shares early Central innovations (ts > cʰ, kṣ > kʰ) but not the geminate package. Its north-western traits (post-nasal voicing, t > l, aspirate devoicing) look areal. Domari and Lomavren are separate, more Central exits.
  • OIA hides little of Nuristani. An oracle that knew which branch of each merged PIIr palatal (ś, j, h, kṣ) to use recovers only 1.5–2.3% of the residual error beyond chance. Part of the j split is even recoverable from OIA vowel context. Sound correspondences also flag about 100 Nuristani etyma as likely Indo-Aryan loans (93% precision on a sanity set).
  • Some changes are shared across distant groups and look areal. Examples: stop + r → a lateral (West Pahari ṭ͡ḷ, Kohistani/Kunar/Pashai ɬ/l, Sauji); s > h before a later c > s that escapes it (Bhil, south-western Rajasthani, Assamese); nasal + voiced stop assimilation before nasal vowels form (Pahari, Bihari, eastern Tharu).
  • Most remaining error is not sound change. Gender and extension suffixes (-ā, -ī, -o, -u) are the largest residual in almost every NIA lect. Citation morphology, compounds, learned vocabulary and mixed transcriptions account for most of the rest. Distinct attested forms of the same etymon in Kohistani already differ from each other by 0.42–0.59 NED.
  • The run surfaced many data problems worth fixing in Jambu. Examples: a Rajasthani wordlist filed under Punjabi, Kannauji forms under Hindi, Domaaki classed as Punjabic when its sound changes are Shinaic, Sanskrit headwords and English/German glosses in reflex fields, and a mangled n/a sequence in several Dardic and West Pahari sources. See the curation list.

How it was built

Data. For every lect with at least 100 etyma linked to a CDIAL Indo-Aryan headword by an inherited reflex edge (borrowings excluded), the reflex pairs were extracted from cldf/forms.csv and cldf/edges.csv: 153 lects, of which the unspecified "MIA" lect was dropped as unusable (fragments and OIA forms) and Sanskrit and Proto-Indo-Iranian were not targets. Nuristani (7 lects) was included as a sister branch. Pairs are split 80/20 by a stable hash of the etymon, so an etymon is in the same split for every lect.

Rules. Each lect has an ordered rule file in a small sound-change language: segments from Jambu's grapheme profile, classes, sets, back-references with feature modifiers ($1-asp, $1+long), environments with optional elements and exceptions, and per-source notation blocks. Every rule carries one or more tags from a shared catalogue (@int-stop-loss, @degem-lengthen …), which is what makes rules comparable across lects.

Metric. Normalised edit distance (NED) over segments between the predicted and the attested form, averaged over reflex pairs, plus exact-match rate. Stress marks, homonym digits and a few lax-vowel letters are normalised on the attested side only.

Process. Twelve agents, one per group of lects, wrote and hill-climbed the chains on the training split only, using per-rule leave-one-out value, residual confusions and a rule-suggestion tool, and kept only rules that read as plausible sound changes. Two baselines: no rules (the etymon itself) and a greedy induction that adds, one at a time, the single-segment change that most reduces training NED. Every chain was then ablated (rules removed greedily, least useful first) to get a rules-vs-score curve.

Chronology. For each pair of rules i before j in a chain, two moves reverse them: j to just before i, and i to just after j. If both raise training NED, the lect requires i before j; if both lower it, the reverse fits better. Results are pooled by tag across lects.

These chains are models of the data, not reconstructions. A rule that improves NED can be a real sound change, a transcription convention, or a regularity of citation form. Tags mark @notation and @morph rules explicitly, and those are excluded from the chronology and grouping analyses.

Scores

The hand-written chains cut test NED from a median {{MED_NULL}} to {{MED_HAND}}. At the same number of rules as the greedy baseline they are better in {{WINS}} of {{N_CMP}} lects; the exceptions (Balkan Romani, Surjapuri, Surgujia, Kvari, Kangri) all have 25–86 test pairs. A staged hypothesis

Putting the pooled orders together with what each group found, the best-supported history of the family's sound changes runs as follows. Stages are relative, and each branch leaves the sequence at a different point.

I. Early MIA (shared by all Indo-Aryan)

ai au > ē ō; r̩ > a/i/u (ri after consonants in Pali, Dardic and some Pahari); loss of final consonants; ty dy > cc jj, which bled the general C + y assimilation (90 lects for, 1 against); the sibilant merger to s, which fed s + stop > aspirated geminate (85 : 1) and kṣ > kkʰ; then assimilation of stop + stop, r + C and C + sonorant into geminates, and the law of two morae. The Kharoṣṭhī north-west (Gāndhārī, Niya) kept the three sibilants and some clusters; Dardic and Nuristani simplified clusters without making geminates.

II. Prakrit lenition

Aspirates > h, ṭ > ḍ, p > v, loss of single intervocalic stops with ya-śruti, n > ṇ. Pali and Aśokan lack all of it; the Kharoṣṭhī texts only voice; Sinhala has its own, later lenition that keeps d and b. Everywhere it applies, it precedes degemination (stop loss: 120 : 0; aspirate > h: 83 : 0; ṭ > ḍ: 114 : 0), so geminates were protected.

III. Late MIA / Apabhraṁśa

Shortening of final -ā (seen in the eastern, Bihari, Hindi, north-western and Marathi chains, always before apocope and before the -aka > -ā contraction); contraction of aya > ē, which the MIA chains show was a verb-paradigm change rather than a phonological one; hiatus contraction after stop loss (126 : 0).

IV. Early NIA

Degemination, with compensatory lengthening in the Central, Eastern and Southern lects (on the penult or the first syllable, depending on the region) and without it in the Northwest, Dardic and Insular. Before degemination, several singleton-only changes had to happen: l > r (Hindi east, Bihari, Sindhi), l > ḷ (Gujarati–Rajasthani, Marathi, Odia, West Pahari), ṇṇ > nn. Nasal + voiced stop assimilation (mb > mm, ŋg > ŋ) happened before nasal vowels formed in Pahari and Bihari. Anusvāra > nasal vowel. The flap ḍ > ṛ came before degemination in the west and after it in Bihari, Bengali and Hindi-Urdu.

V. Late NIA

Loss of final short vowels, after compensatory lengthening (40 : 2); merger of i/u length after lengthening in the eastern lects; syncope (before or after apocope depending on region); v- > b-, y- > j-, ṇ > n in the Hindi and eastern area; s > h in Bhil, south-western Rajasthani, the Northwest, Assamese and Dhivehi, which in three separate groups is older than a later c > s.

Shared changes and subgroups

The maps show where some of the defining changes are present in the final chains (South Asian lects only). They reproduce the textbook isoglosses: no compensatory lengthening across the Northwest and Dardic, post-nasal voicing in the Northwest and Dardic, s > h in the south-west and Assam, and the sibilant merger to ś only in the Bengal–Assam corner.

Dardic (south)

Northwest

Punjabi, Sindhi, Lahnda, Old Punjabi, Hindko, Kutchi, Domaaki, Saraiki, Dhatki, Pothwari, Khetrani, Kholosi · Sindhi 0.352, Punjabi 0.359

West Pahari

16 lects · Jaunsari 0.404, Kiunthali 0.431, Kullui 0.438, Kotgarhi 0.448

Central and Eastern Pahari, Romani

Hindi belt

Hindi 0.362 (18% exact), Old Awadhi 0.260 (34%), Awadhi 0.309, Braj 0.436 · 10 lects

  • The sharpest isogloss is intervocalic l > r, and it is not West vs East: Braj, Bundeli, Old Awadhi, Awadhi, Chhattisgarhi and Surgujia have it; standard Hindi and Haryanvi keep l. It precedes degemination, so old -ll- stays l.
  • Old Awadhi is an older stage of Awadhi: the same choices in the same order, lacking only Awadhi's last change (a second flap after nasal vowels), which Braj shares.
  • Haryanvi alone has n > ṇ and h-loss with tone, both Panjabi-side features.
  • 228 Hindi words resist compensatory lengthening (pakkā, billā, ḍabbā): an expressive or suffixed stratum.

Rajasthani and Gujarati

18 lects · Old Gujarati 0.313, Old Marwari 0.319, Gujarati 0.373

  • A Gujarati branch (Gujarati, Old Gujarati, Wadiyara Koli) shares medial syncope before apocope and the -vũ infinitive; only Gujarati and Old Gujarati keep tr.
  • l > ḷ, the flap, ṇṇ > nn and s > h all precede degemination; compensatory lengthening precedes apocope (up to +0.056).
  • s > h is south-western only (Godwari 88%, Mewari 58%) plus Koli; Ahirwadi is Hindi-like; -vv- > -bb- splits east from west.
  • Gujari is an outlier: no compensatory lengthening, no n > ṇ, nasal clusters kept; read as a Rajasthani base reshaped by north-western contact.

Marathi, Konkani and Bhil

17 lects · Marathi 0.372, Halbi 0.339, Konkani 0.452

  • The Bhil lects are Gujarati–Rajasthani in type (masculine -o, s > h, nasal clusters kept); the Nimadi chain fits them better than the Marathi one.
  • In Noiri, Dungra Bhil, Wagri and Pauri Bareli, s > h precedes c > s: the new s escaped debuccalisation.
  • Halbi and Bhatri side with Hindi and Odia; nothing specifically Marathi survives.
  • Compensatory lengthening fits the first syllable, not the penult, in Marathi and most Bhil lects.
  • Old Marathi (Tulpule) is mostly learned vocabulary (18% of pairs within 0.2 NED of the Sanskrit), so it gives no chronology.

Bihari and Tharu

17 lects · Bhojpuri 0.298 (30% exact), Maithili 0.337, Bihari 0.391

  • No Magadhan ś anywhere in the data.
  • Nasal + voiced stop: kept in western Tharu; assimilated early in the Bihari core (before nasal vowels); NN in eastern Tharu, Danuwar and Majhi (bandʰa > bān).

Eastern

Odia 0.322, Bengali 0.336, Assamese 0.353, Bishnupriya 0.247 (38% exact)

Things worth a closer look

  1. aya > ē is morphological. Restricting it to -ayati/-ayatē verbs beats the general rule in Prakrit, Pali and Gāndhārī, and Aśokan lacks it altogether.
  2. Kharoṣṭhī lenition is graded by register: Gāndhārī Dharmapada > Niya documents > inscriptions (none), and all stop at voicing.
  3. Insular lenition is not Prakritic. Sinhala keeps d and b, and loses intervocalic d before j > d; Old Sinhala shows no stop loss at all.
  4. Khowar vowel harmony. First-syllable a > o when the next syllable has short a (95 : 36), which replaced an accent-based rule; the data also suggest rounding before u/ō and i > e before i. {{DN_HARMONY}}
  5. A Cr > lateral isogloss links West Pahari (ṭ͡ḷ), Kohistani/Kunar/Pashai (ɬ, l) and Sauji (tr > ɬ, dr > l), and West Pahari tr > c goes through it.
  6. pr > ʦ̣ in Indus Kohistani and Bhateri (pralabʰatē > ʦ̣ālǎṽ), implying a general *Cr > retroflex affricate before the labial was lost.
  7. The -aka final vowel survives in Kohistani only where apocope preceded hiatus contraction, an order that splits the Swat lects from Indus Kohistani and Gowro.
  8. West Pahari a > ɔ is weight-conditioned, blocked before a following long ā (kaṭāha > kəṛā́) and older than degemination.
  9. Hindi l > r is not a West/East split: Braj goes with Awadhi, and Khari Boli with Haryanvi keeps l.
  10. Stress-conditioned lengthening before anusvāra in Gujarati (ā̃gaṇ, kā̃ṭɔ vs ãgār, ãdʰārũ), and sk/ṣk > unaspirated kk in several Rajasthani lects, which looks like the Prakrit sukka ~ sukkha doublet surviving.
  11. Counter-feeding s > h before c > s recurs in three independent areas (Bhil, south-western Rajasthani/Koli, Assamese).
  12. Sindhi degemination is later than its implosives, and gemination in the Northwest is partly a transcription variable: CDIAL writes geminates in 13–17% of Punjabi/Lahnda reflexes, the phonetic wordlists almost never.
  13. -triṁśat numerals keep tr in every eastern lect (Bengali ektris, Assamese ektrix, Odia ekatrisa), against Hindi ikattīs: a learned or compound-boundary effect.
  14. Western Tharu keeps voiced nasal clusters and has weak lengthening, matching an Awadhi–Bhojpuri affiliation, while eastern Tharu, Danuwar and Majhi share the Bengali–Assamese NN outcome.
  15. Two-mora shortening is invisible in NIA in four separate groups, for the same reason each time: compensatory lengthening restores the length it removed.

Nuristani: what OIA can predict

Nuristani is not descended from OIA, so these chains are correspondence rules read from the OIA side. They still behave like a single ordered history, and they give a direct answer to how much of Nuristani is inferable from OIA.

The palatal problem is small

OIA merged PIIr contrasts that Nuristani keeps: ś (Nuristani ʦ, or ś in loans), j (ʣ or j), h (ʣ or j) and kṣ (three outcomes). An oracle that picks the right branch per etymon recovers 2.3% of the residual error in Proto-Nuristani, 1.9% in Kati and 1.5% in Kamviri and Waigali, after subtracting what a control oracle over meaningless alternatives gains by chance. About a third of the affected etyma would be better with the other branch, but they are few. The residual is dominated by derivation and compounds in the cited forms, unpredictable vowels (Kati and Kamviri ā ~ o, u ~ ɨ) and mixed transcriptions.

Part of the j split is recoverable from OIA itself: j > ʣ before a/ā, with j kept before front vowels, beats an unconditioned rule in four lects. OIA j before front vowels mostly continues PIIr *ǰ from the second palatalisation.

Loans and inherited words

CDIAL's Nuristani links are stored as neutral groupings. Diagnostic sounds (ś kept rather than ʦ, aspirates, h > ʣ, j > ʣ, cʰ > ʦ) classify about 100 etyma as likely Indo-Aryan loans and 160 as inherited. On a sanity set, 99.6% of strict "inherited" verdicts are core vocabulary and 93% of strict "loan" verdicts (numerals aside) are culture words: oath, guest, country, adze, trap, spear, stable, rice, cat. Loan rates are similar across lects (about 30%); the difference between sources is larger (Turner/Morgenstierne 26–42%, Strand 18–27%), which suggests Turner linked Nuristani words mainly when they looked Indo-Aryan. Whole-chain comparison against Dardic chains barely separates the strata (AUC 0.53–0.57). Caveats: initial ś- before a liquid may be inherited (śarád), Persian loans mimic the archaisms (sahásra > āzor), and wanderwörter are classified by shape only.

Romani and Domari

Verdict: Central origin, early exit, then a north-western period. Replacing a migratory chain's MIA block with the Hindi one costs +0.087 (European Romani), +0.066 (Welsh), +0.071 (Balkan), +0.072 (Lomavren), +0.060 (Zargari) and +0.054 (Domari). Romani shares with Central/NW MIA the innovations ts/ps > cʰ, medial kṣ > kʰ, ava > o, r̩ > i/u and medial syncope, none of which are Dardic. It shares with Dardic only retentions: s + stop and stop + r kept (trin, drak, pʰral), no gemination, no nasal vowels.

The dental lenition is the key clue. Intervocalic t/d became l (Domari r) rather than disappearing, and this had to happen before clusters simplified (+0.003 to +0.008), so geminate tt stayed t (mattá > mato). The ancestors left after -t- had weakened but before the Central loss of intervocalic stops. The north-western traits (post-nasal voicing, found in 0 of 10 Central chains but 10 of 12 Northwest; aspirate devoicing with Pothwari and Dogri parallels; ṣ > ś with s kept, as in West Pahari) are the kind of change easily shared through contact.

Whole-chain scores alone do not settle this: raw scores favour the Kalasha chain, but after normalising length, nasal vowels and geminates, the Hindi, Punjabi, Lahnda and Shina chains all fit European Romani within about 0.01. Domari is the most Central in its obstruents (s-merger, velar loss) and lacks the Romani aspirate devoicing (adding it costs +0.02); its closest outside chain is Domaaki. Lomavren adds Central/NW changes Romani lacks (st > tʰ, voiced aspirate > h), consistent with a later exit.

Data problems for curation

The agents recorded these while working. None were changed.

Mislabelled lects and mixed sources

  • Domaaki (D) is classed Punjabic in languages.csv, but its sound changes are Shinaic.
  • Punjabi / bagri (130 pairs) is a Rajasthani (Bagri) wordlist; Hindi / kannauji (404) is Kannauji dialect data; Bengali / zoller2023 is Bangani (a code collision on "B"); Odia and Adivasi Oriya / beine2017bhatri is Bhatri.
  • "Malvi" includes a Mewari village list (Basad); "Rj" is Mewari survey data; "Nimadi" includes Bareli (a Bhil language); "Gujari" mixes Pakistani and Eastern Gujari with Noira; the Marathi "bundeli" source is a Marathi-like wordlist.
  • The unspecified "MIA" lect (119 etyma) is fragments, OIA forms and unrelated look-ups.
  • Pashai CDIAL forms are a dialect mixture (44% of etyma have two or more distinct forms).

Wrong content in reflex fields

  • Sanskrit forms as reflexes: vásati, upaviṣṭa (Or, B, A under úpaviśati); ávadahati (A, Mth under *uddahati); Gujarati saṁlōḍyatē, nárāśáṁsa, útkasati; Kumaoni śvaśrūśvaśura; Prasun dvā́daśa.
  • Glosses as reflexes: Kiunthali vitʰout ("without"), European Romani branntvein, Prasun vr̩ṣabʰṓ jyā́yān; Zoller part-of-speech abbreviations in Indus Kohistani reflexes.
  • Encoding debris: a mangled n/a for a vowel letter (18 Kotgarhi pairs, also Bhalesi, Khashali, Kalasha, Shina, Gawarbati, Dameli, Palula); raw <sup> in 11 CDIAL Kashmiri forms and some Dardic ones; &gt; in a Nepali reflex.
  • Fragments and phrases: Lahnda sárva → a, European Romani karambʰá → u, phrases in Grierson's Bihari and several survey lists.

Transcription and tooling

  • Several field sources need source-level notation metadata (written length, dentals written with the retroflex underdot, digraph aspirates); the agents' %source blocks record them.
  • The CDIAL profile splits accented diphthongs (saínya, saúvarṇa; 31 sequences among 18,527 etyma), and U+01DD ǝ is not folded like ə.
  • Nuristani loan/inherited candidates are in results/nuristani-strata.csv for review.

Limits and next steps

  • Morphology is the ceiling. Gender and extension suffixes, infinitive and other citation forms, and compounds dominate the residual. The most-requested engine feature (7 of 12 groups) is conditioning rules on part of speech or word class; next are source blocks that can run inside the chain (6) and morpheme boundaries (5).
  • Non-local contexts are hard to find. The rule-suggestion tool only proposes changes conditioned on adjacent segments, so vowel harmony, umlaut and stress effects were found only when someone looked for them (Khowar, Kalasha, West Pahari, Gujarati). A correspondence-table view conditioned on the next syllable would help every lect.
  • Small test sets. About 60 lects have fewer than 100 test pairs; their test NED moves by ±0.01–0.05 with single words. Train numbers are steadier.
  • Orders are only as good as the chains. An order is evidence only where both single-rule moves hurt, and rules phrased differently can change which orders are testable (for example, lengthening conditioned on a following vowel versus a word-final geminate).
  • Grouping. The tag clustering uses presence and absence, so shared absences pull lects together. Cross-scoring chains on other lects' data, which several groups did locally, is a better tree-building signal but needs notation normalisation.
