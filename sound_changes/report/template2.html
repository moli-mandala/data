Subgroups from shared rule-list prefixes

Every rule in a chain is an innovation, a change away from OIA; a retention is only the absence of a rule, so nothing here can group lects by what they kept. Each lect's phonological rules (notation, morphology, accent-loss and accent-placement rules left out) are its innovations, each identified by the exact rule: nothing is pooled by tag, so s > h and s > h / V _ V, or kṣ > kkʰ and kṣ > ʦ̣ʰ, are different innovations. All chains are put in one canonical order, the innovations shared by more lects first (the order in which a perfect phylogeny branches), and the tree is built from how long a prefix of that list two lects share.

A strict prefix breaks a lect off at the first widespread innovation it lacks, and with {{PT_NSHARED}} shared innovations {{PT_HOMO}} of them end up on more than one branch, so the strict tree cannot be read. The tree below lets a prefix survive {{PT_TOL}} disagreements and clusters lects by the length of the prefix they share (average linkage). Each node lists its defining innovations: present in ≥ 70% of its lects and ≤ 20% of the others. Caveat: below the top levels the tree largely reproduces the twelve agent groups, whose chains share wording as well as history; read the structure between groups, not within them.

Relative chronology

For every pair of rules in a chain, both single-rule moves that reverse them were tried on the training data. When both made the fit worse, that lect counts as evidence for the order; when both made it better, as evidence against. A change here is one definition in the central file changes.sc, used verbatim by every lect that has it (or, for a rule found in one lect only, that exact rule). Nothing is pooled by tag, so s > h and s > h / V _ V are different changes; differently written rules were merged only when they behave identically on every word. Pick a rule to see what the data say must come before and after it across all 151 chains.

Round 2: vowels and the Vedic accent

{{R2_TEXT}}

What the Vedic accent does and does not condition

Every group tested the same questions: is the accent a better conditioner than syllable position or weight? A cell is + when an accent rule was adopted because it beat the accent-blind control on held-out training words, ~ when the effect is partial or tiny, − when every accent version was worse, 0 when rates were flat. Click a cell for the numbers.

Where does the modern accent come from?

A second score, accent NED, keeps the accent. It is computed only on attested forms whose source marks one, reading acute, grave, circumflex and caron alike as "this vowel carries the accent": the ordinary edit distance plus half an edit when the accent is missing or misplaced. In the engine the accent lives on a separate tier that no rule can see, so placing it never changes the ordinary NED. The chain's accent-loss rule hides the Vedic accent instead of deleting it, and the accent then travels with its vowel through every later change. That makes a direct test possible: does the Vedic accent, carried through each lect's own derivation, predict the modern accent better than a positional rule?

Six group agents hill-climbed the accent score for every lect with accent-marked forms. The answer is the same everywhere. Carrying the Vedic accent through beats no accent by at most 0.01; a positional rule beats it by 0.03–0.12. The rules adopted are the old penult placed before apocope, or the last syllable after it (often the same syllable), in Shina, Palula, Kalasha, Khowar, Kohistani, Pashai, Nuristani and Domari; the first syllable in Kotgarhi, Bhalesi and Bashkarik; the first heavy syllable in Hindko. Aspiration shifts the accent rightward in the raw Palula and Shina data (56% vs 45% and 55% vs 36% on σ2 after an initial voiced aspirate), but after apocope the shift has nothing left to act on. The Vedic accent's one clear trace is segmental: Palula first-syllable ā becomes o/ō in 52% of barytones but 12% of oxytones.

Score change per lect

Scores

Test NED (normalised edit distance to the attested form; lower is better) for every lect, with no rules, with the hand-written chain, and with the chain scored extension-aware.

Extension-aware score

More than half of the remaining error sits in the word ending, and most of it is the choice of MIA stem extension or gender ending (-∅ vs -ā/-ī/-o, -ṛā/-lā/-rā). The extension-aware NED also runs each headword through the chain with every extension on a fixed menu (-aka, -akka, -ikā, -ikka, -iyaka, -uka, -aḍaka, -allaka, -araka, neuter -akam, replacing the final vowel); verbs instead get a menu of OIA verb formations on the root (present -ati, verbal nouns -anaka/-aṇa/-anīya, gerundive -tavya, participle -ita, infinitive -itum, bare root), so that the chain must derive each lect's citation form and scores each attested form against its closest variant, one choice per reflex since several endings coexist. No cost is charged for an extension, so it is an optimistic score, shown next to the plain NED above (orange-red dots) and never instead of it. The chart shows which endings the reflexes choose.
