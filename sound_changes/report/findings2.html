• The Vedic accent left almost no trace in NIA vowels. All 12 groups tested accent against syllable position and weight for pretonic shortening, lengthening, apocope, syncope, r̩ and vowel quality. Position or weight wins nearly everywhere. Compensatory lengthening follows the first syllable (Marathi, Bhil, Eastern, Rajasthani–Gujarati) or the modern stressed syllable (Hindi belt), never the Vedic accent. What survives is small and scattered: Hindi final -u > -ū except under the accent (áśru > ā̃sū, bāhú > bāh), oxytone -í kept through apocope in western Tharu, Pashai lengthening of the accented vowel in disyllables, and a Palula drag chain in which only accented ā becomes ō. Khowar does the opposite of Tharu, losing accented final -i/-u, so even the final-vowel effect has no single direction. A second, accent-aware score settles where the modern accent comes from: in all 32 lects whose sources mark accent, a positional rule (the old penult before apocope, the last syllable after it, or the first syllable) beats the Vedic accent carried through the chain by 0.03–0.12; the Vedic accent beats no accent by at most 0.01. See the accent grid and accent table.
  • Subgroups from shared rule-list prefixes (innovations only, rules never pooled). Each chain lists only changes away from OIA, so a retention can never group lects, and a change is one exact rule, so differently conditioned rules (s > h vs s > h / V _ V) never count as shared. Putting every chain's rules in one canonical order (most widespread first) and asking how long a prefix two lects share gives a tree whose first split separates Dardic + Romani/Domari (plain degemination, anusvāra lost) from the rest; within the rest, early MIA (Pali, Aśokan, Kharoṣṭhī) separates from a mainland defined by C + r/ḣ and stop + stop assimilation and intervocalic stop loss. The strict prefix tree is too fragile to read, and below the top levels the tree largely reproduces the agent groups, whose chains share wording as well as history. See the prefix tree.
  • Round 2 found a few new shared innovations in the vowels. Medial u > a helps all 8 core Bhil lects and hurts every other lect, a clean Bhil-core isogloss. Kati and Kamviri share two vowel shifts (ā > o in the new final syllable, a > u in monosyllables) that every other Nuristani lect rejects. Romani a > e in open first syllables (blocked before labials) unites Welsh, Balkan and Zargari against Lomavren and Domari. Odia alone nasalises V m V (kumāra > kuā̃ra).
  • One family-wide skeleton of relative chronology comes out of the data. Across all 151 chains, the order of pairs of changes was tested by moving each rule past the other. The cluster assimilations and sibilant merger come first. Intervocalic lenition follows, then degemination and compensatory lengthening, then loss of final vowels. Counting each lect's own rules, intervocalic stop loss must precede degemination in 120 lects with none against, and compensatory lengthening must precede apocope in 45 lects with 2 against. At the level of single changes (one definition shared across lects in changes.sc; nothing pooled by tag), int-stop-loss.kgcjtdy ({k g c j t d y} > ∅ / V _ V) precedes degem-plain.g (G $1 > $2) in 66 lects with none against, and the sibilant merger Sib > s precedes kṣ > kkʰ in 80 with none against.
  • Lenition before cluster reduction holds in every branch. What differs is how clusters reduce. Mainland MIA made geminates, which resisted lenition and simplified much later. Dardic, Nuristani and (partly) Romani never geminated, but their clusters still simplified only after lenition (41 lects for, 0 against, counting each lect's own rules). This is the clearest structural split between Dardic and the mainland.
  • The MIA stages separate cleanly. Pali and Aśokan reject every Prakrit lenition (adding intervocalic stop loss costs +0.058 NED). The Kharoṣṭhī texts keep all three sibilants and lenite only by voicing, in decreasing degree from the Gāndhārī Dharmapada to the Niya documents to the inscriptions. Sinhala and Dhivehi start from a Pali-like stage and develop their own lenition.
  • Where compensatory lengthening falls is a major isogloss. It is limited to disyllables or the penult in Hindi, Pahari and Gujarati; to the initial syllable in Marathi, the Bhil lects, Bihari, Bengali, Assamese and Odia, where round 2 also found it blocked before a heavy syllable in Rajasthani–Gujarati; absent in the Northwest, Dardic, Insular and Romani. Adding it to any Northwest lect costs +0.03 to +0.07 NED.
  • Several classic subgroupings fall out of the rule files without being built in. Examples: Kamarupi (Assamese, Kamta, Rajbanshi, Surjapuri, Hajong, Bishnupriya) by ḍ > r; Central vs Eastern Pahari; Shina proper by tr > ʦ̣; the Northwest by no lengthening + post-nasal voicing + rt > ṭṭ; the Bhil lects as Gujarati–Rajasthani rather than Marathi; Halbi and Bhatri as central/eastern rather than Marathi.
  • Romani left the Central continuum early and then spent time in the Northwest. Grafting the Hindi MIA block onto any Romani chain costs +0.054 to +0.087 NED. Romani shares early Central innovations (ts > cʰ, kṣ > kʰ) but not the geminate package. Its north-western traits (post-nasal voicing, t > l, aspirate devoicing) look areal. Domari and Lomavren are separate, more Central exits.
  • OIA hides little of Nuristani. An oracle that knew which branch of each merged PIIr palatal (ś, j, h, kṣ) to use recovers only 1.5–2.3% of the residual error beyond chance. Part of the j split is even recoverable from OIA vowel context. Sound correspondences also flag about 100 Nuristani etyma as likely Indo-Aryan loans (93% precision on a sanity set).
  • Some changes are shared across distant groups and look areal. Examples: stop + r → a lateral (West Pahari ṭ͡ḷ, Kohistani/Kunar/Pashai ɬ/l, Sauji); s > h before a later c > s that escapes it (Bhil, south-western Rajasthani, Assamese); nasal + voiced stop assimilation before nasal vowels form (Pahari, Bihari, eastern Tharu).
  • Most remaining error is not sound change. Gender and extension suffixes (-ā, -ī, -o, -u) are the largest residual in almost every NIA lect. Citation morphology, compounds, learned vocabulary and mixed transcriptions account for most of the rest. Distinct attested forms of the same etymon in Kohistani already differ from each other by 0.42–0.59 NED.
  • The run surfaced many data problems worth fixing in Jambu. Examples: a Rajasthani wordlist filed under Punjabi, Kannauji forms under Hindi, Domaaki classed as Punjabic when its sound changes are Shinaic, Sanskrit headwords and English/German glosses in reflex fields, and a mangled n/a sequence in several Dardic and West Pahari sources.
